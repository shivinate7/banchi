# p-seasons

**Name:** Season Clock

**The idea:** Keep a forward calendar of known events: set releases, prerelease weekends, format rotation dates, big tournaments, holidays and school breaks. Line each event up against the store's own past orders, so each one comes with a learned effect, such as "the last two releases tripled orders for 10 days, mostly from the newest 3 sets." Before each event the store shows what is likely coming and what to prepare.

**Who it helps:** The owner, who plans stock and prices. The Fulfiller, who needs to know their hours and supplies a week ahead, not on the day.

**Why now:** A median of 3 orders against a peak of 39 means the dangerous days are the rare ones. The order history is still small enough to label by hand, and long enough to show the pattern.

**Rough shape:**
- A strip of the next 8 weeks, with events as markers and an expected-orders band under them.
- Each event opens a short prep card: "Expect 15–25/day from Dec 12. Stock 60 mailers, 200 sleeves. Fulfiller: 2 extra sessions."
- The Fulfiller sees only their part: which days are busy and what to have on hand.
- After the event, the guess is set beside what happened, so the next guess gets better.

**Risk:** With few past seasons, the forecast may look more certain than it is. Show it as a range, never as one number.

**Name:** Front-of-Box Staging

**The idea:** The store knows where every card sits, so before a spike it can move the cards most likely to sell to the front sections of each box. These are cards from a set whose sequel releases next week, or cards in a deck that just won a tournament. Peak-day picking then becomes a short walk through a few sections, not a hunt across 6 long boxes.

**Who it helps:** Mainly the Fulfiller, on the 39-order days, when slow picks turn into late shipments. The owner can also see what is staged and why.

**Why now:** Pick time grows with the number of orders. A spike is exactly when the 9–22 sections per box start to cost real minutes. The location record already exists, so this uses it for something new.

**Rough shape:**
- Before an event, a "stage these 40 cards" pull list appears. It is a walk ordered by box and section, the same as a pick.
- After the move, each staged card's new location is confirmed in one tap per card.
- After the season, a "return to home" walk puts cards back in set order, or leaves them if they keep selling.

**Risk:** Each move is a chance to lose a card. The return walk must be as reliable as the pick.

---

**Name:** Reprint and Rotation Weather

**The idea:** Some news moves a whole group of cards at once. A reprint announcement or a rotation date usually lowers prices. A viral moment or a ban usually raises them. This direction watches for that news and proposes one batch reprice for the affected group, ready the day the news lands. The owner approves one sweep, not 200 single edits.

**Who it helps:** The owner, who decides prices. It protects the few high-value cards and slabs, where one stale price costs real money.

**Why now:** Cards that only take bulk attention are the ones left at last month's price when a reprint drops. At 3,600 cards, a few outdated prices on valuable cards can erase a month's margin.

**Rough shape:**
- A news feed, with each item marked by the cards it touches: "Reprint announced: 14 of your cards, $310 listed value."
- One proposed action per item, such as "list now before the drop" or "hold, raise 20%", with the expected effect.
- Approval produces the marketplace upload file for just that group.
- A short record of each sweep, and what the prices did afterwards.

**Risk:** Linking a news item to the right cards and printings is the same matching problem as identification. A wrong link reprices the wrong card.

---

**Bet:** I would build Season Clock first. It needs only the order history the store already has plus a hand-kept list of events. It makes the other two directions possible, because staging and repricing both need to know when the next wave arrives. It also turns the 39-order day from a surprise into a planned day.
