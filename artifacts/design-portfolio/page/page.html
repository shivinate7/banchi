Process

How the mark got locked

Every ground is derived, not picked — the prism's own hue, driven dark. What follows is the actual sequence: what was swept, what was measured, what got dropped.

01

The ground rule

Locked

The ground isn't a free color next to the prism — it's the prism's own hue, driven down to near-black. One formula reproduces every published stop across bluesteel, lilacish and mint.

02

Which drop

Locked

Flat and inverted shown and dropped — one has no bottom to fall to, the other only worked by two effects cancelling out. Held and cooler both kept: both work, so both became live variants rather than one answer with a runner-up.

03

Brackets, measured

Locked

Not eyeballed: plain gold's darkest stop lands at L 0.606, right where the bracket tapers to nothing. Pale gold was the only candidate that cleared chrome's own floor there.

04

Black & gold

Locked

The formula's own answer for a gold ground was an espresso brown — reads dirty, not black. Rejected for true black: have pale gold with true black as our locked option.

05

White & rose gold — and the lock

Locked

Cool white gold turned out to be bluesteel — dropped for colliding with an existing member. Rose gold's marbling reads as coral rather than metal, flagged as a real limit rather than patched. Row 5 is where all six were called: lock all on #5; we'll have the bluesteel one be the default.

06

The small cut

Locked

The display mark's taper vanishes below 64px. Stroke swept 2.4 / 3.0 / 4.2 / 5.0, no taper, no filter — 4.2 ships in the rail and the favicon.

07

A light ground

Rejected

Derivable, and handsome at 150px. But every surface in this app draws the mark under 64px, and at real size the light tile loses its silhouette — a smudge, not an object. The mark stays fixed dark in both themes.

The four interaction floorsapp/src/base.css — element selectors, not per-screen rules
button, select, summary, [role='button'] { cursor: pointer; } [aria-disabled='true'], :disabled { cursor: not-allowed; } button, select, summary, a, [role='tab'] { transition: background-color 120ms, border-color 120ms, color 120ms, box-shadow 120ms; } button:active, a:active, [role='button']:active { translate: 0 1px; }
Cursor, response, press — all three are live on this page, because this page uses the real rules.

The press dip is translate, never transform: translateY — the two compose, so spelling it as a transform silently doubled the dip to 2px in six rules. And a control may never ease the movement its own :active makes: the dip lands on the frame the finger goes down, and a squeeze on a 120ms transition beside it is one gesture on two clocks. The fourth floor — a press may not move what is around it — can't be written as a stylesheet rule at all; it is enforced by two test suites instead. See the UX Decisions tab.

D121

The front page says what is owed

Rejected 4×

The old lede counted the store: "100 cards on hand in 4 boxes · 19 sold." Six replacement variants went up in one pass. All six were turned down at once.

The diagnosis was structural, not cosmetic: every variant restated an aggregate that was already drawn by the spine 24px below, already known to the store's only author, and unchanged between most two openings. Duplicated, already known and static is not information — it is furniture.

The test the replacement had to pass: does reading it change what the operator does in the next ten minutes. What survived was a ranked standing line — head of the queue, verb in it, a coloured left rule for whose turn it is.

Then the owner amended it: the page still could not show the library at a glance. Totals came back — but as context below the button, behind a hairline, achromatic. Never as the headline. Settled after five passes.

The library is drawn as the work that made it: sittings clustered from capture timestamps, each block as wide as its minutes and as tall as its cards-an-hour, so its area is its card count. The geometry reads back 543 / 172 / 152 / 138 / 555 / 65 — the true counts.

D118

A press changes what is on the screen, never where the rest of it is

Shipped

Measured at 1440×900: one Mark sold collapsed the card panel 98px and moved 131 elements. Stepping the box walk moved 39, 66 or 98px depending on which two cards were involved. Every other route: zero.

Four causes. Three were facts about whichever card was selected — the position lens (85px), the action slot (18px), the copies list. The fourth was found by the guard rather than by eye: a row auto-placing a fourth child onto a second grid row, 32px → 50px the moment a card sold.

The fix is a band height bounded by the viewport, not by the card — because every other candidate for that number is a fact about the selection, and the selection is what was moving.

Asked how a departed copy should leave its row, the owner said "a sentence or even better an animation" — so the row is marked and animates out rather than being deleted, because a deleted node cannot animate.

D50

An element's feedback is the product's, not each stylesheet's

Shipped

Three floors, built in stages. Cursor: of 65 control shapes, 41 had a wrong cursor — and the concentration was the disabled state, where 30 clickables said pointer while refusing the click. Missing focus rings, the thing everybody would have guessed: zero.

Response: hovering all 315 controls with the transition settled, 293 responded, 17 snapped, 22 were dead. Ten of the seventeen were one element — the brand block, drawn once per route. The floor took snaps to 0 of 294 without a single per-screen edit.

Press: 121 of 311 controls answered a finger; 190 were silent, the largest group being every sidebar nav link. After: 317 of 317.

Rejected along the way: transition: all; and a flat gradient hover layer that shipped, passed the guard, and still snapped — because background-image is not animatable, so it lands in one frame however it is transitioned. The entry leaves the earlier wrong sentence visibly struck rather than deleted, "because it is the thing that was wrong."

D110

A hover is an alpha, because one paint over three grounds is three hovers

Shipped

One token was the hover everywhere. Measured in CIE L*, it landed on three different grounds as ΔL* 8.67 on the sidebar, 7.70 on the page, 4.00 on a panel — a spread of 4.67. 4.00 is a good hover; 8.67 reads as selection, competing with the accent tint two rows below it.

As a 4% white alpha instead: 3.61 / 4.22 / 4.51 — spread 0.90. An alpha composites against whatever is beneath it, so no element has to know its own ground.

The rules split by a real distinction: 31 rules whose element rests transparent take the alpha; 12 that paint their own ground keep theirs — and every one of those 12 independently carries a lift or a shadow, which is the same claim made a second way.

Three dark-only overrides were deleted, two of which hovered down — drawn as dark holes punched in a panel.

D120

The shell speaks one brand at every width

Shipped

The product wore two brands, and which one you saw depended on window width: the 番地/BANCHI lockup in the sidebar above 1023px, and a tile plus wordmark in the top bar — plus tile, wordmark and tagline in the drawer — below 768. Three brand objects in one shell, with the dark tile very nearly disappearing on the glass surface.

Putting the real lockup in the top bar was rejected with arithmetic: its floor is a 102×75 block against a 52px bar, and a bar tall enough would be 84px — 10% of an 844px viewport spent on a brand row, on every screen. So the phone bar draws exactly what the 64px rail draws.

Why nothing caught it: the nav spec scoped to the sidebar, and the cursor sweep harvested its routes from sidebar links — which are display:none below 768, so it could not run at a phone width even in principle. Of twenty specs, one set a phone viewport. The shell had no test at any width.

D117

The thumb floor is the kit's, and the measurement is the hit area

Shipped

Two phone floors had been published since the rebrand and neither had a reader. Twenty-five controls hand-rolled a mouse-sized one; the checkbox had no height at all — 19.5px at 390px wide — and two sheets had quietly lifted private copies of it.

The arm is the pointer, not the width. Ten stylesheets carried width-only blocks, so an iPad in portrait — 820px, and all thumb — got none of them.

The measurement is the hit area, not the box, and that is what removes the allow-list: ticks draw at 22px and take the tap at 46 via a negative-inset pseudo-element. A box-only assertion needs a forgiveness list, and a list of names is a list somebody adds to.

On the pricing screen, the ship bar stood 430px of an 844px viewport with the control it gates underneath it — visible, and impossible to press at any scroll position. The obvious fix, padding the page by the bar height, was built and then refused: it added 172px of dead scroll under a list the operator walks.

D159

The band is copies rather than SKUs, and the drawer is the first answer

Shipped

Asked to brainstorm this all rather than receive it, so the store was measured first. Across 2,245 cards on hand: median $0.23, mean $1.36, top card $47.74. The top 5% is 92 cards carrying 53.8% of the value; the bottom 56% is 1,042 cards carrying 5.7%.

The load-bearing measurement: value clusters by box and scatters within one. Box 4 is 65% of the money. But contiguity runs 1.40 cards per reach above $5, against 6.51 below the cut-off — so one control with two ends: the rich end is a pick list with an address per row, the cheap end is not a list at all, because the honest instruction is take drawer 2 and drawer 5.

The unit is the copy, not the SKU: the 122 cards above $5 are only 38 SKUs, and a per-SKU list draws a third of the rows and a third of the drawers a hand has to open.

390 of 2,245 have no market price — each is still a row, tagged with one of three causes, because a screen that collapsed them would prescribe one thing for three different problems.

Shipped

D123

Browser zoom is not the lever it looks like

Half deferred

The owner noticed their coding sessions looked way nicer at like 80% zoom rather than 100% and asked whether the app should be denser. Half the answer is no: zoom is a similarity transform — 80% is every length ×0.8 and every breakpoint ×1.25. There is no rendering quality to reclaim. A density token is deferred on the record rather than silently dropped.

The other half is a real defect, and zoom makes it worse. Across 31 stylesheets there were 124 media blocks and 16 container blocks — and above 1024px, only 13 rules in 7 files. The single widest breakpoint in the product hid a class no component renders.

What that costs, on the pricing screen at 1920: nine of ten tracks are fixed, so the card's name was 924px wide while the price field it exists to decide was 68px.

D95

The Fulfiller's crash page has no door out

Shipped

The app has two personas and one of them — the person pulling cards to ship — gets no shell at all: no nav, no palette, no theme toggle. Not hidden; not rendered, so it is not focusable, not reachable by a screen reader, and not one specificity change from coming back.

That extends to the error boundary. His crash page offers one control, and it reopens the screen he is on, because the constraints table reads zero routes reachable from this view and a crash is the moment he is most likely to press whatever is offered.

Before this, the shared boundary drew a "Go home" link at 14px — below his own type floor, and a door into the pipeline reachable only by the screen breaking.

Also rejected here: restoring the inline arrow-key hints the rebuild had dropped. One complete reference sheet on ? beats a hint on one screen that isn't.

D155

The section is the ruler, the box is the margin note

Shipped

Two sentences from the owner: the box view is nicer than section, and the lens between the two scales is more noise than useful. Read as praise of structure, not size — a flip that only made the section taller would have answered the wrong half.

Justified by resolution: the box track draws 1.45px per card on the owner's bar, and the mark's own footprint covers about 7.6 cards — while the caption 3px above already says #51 of 53 · 96% in exactly. The section scale runs 6.8px to 82.9px per card.

The trapezoid lens was three defects, two of them unfixable in principle: its asymmetry is scale-invariant, and its dominant output was a tint band in the same token as the track 3px below it. The third — a percentage calculation that had to agree with a flex layout — drifted 8px on 5 sections and 42px on a 22-section box.

Total height is unchanged at 73px, so no row moves a pixel — which is D118's rule applied to a redesign rather than to a press.

The old app was measured before anything was proposed, then three competing first principles were put through an adversarial evidence pass against real prior art. Two were refuted on their own numbers.

A

The Rig — one instrument, three fixed docks

Survives as convention

"A tool you spend hours in should never change address."

A frame that never reflows — rail, stage, dock — whatever the subject. The best-evidenced structure in the corpus.

Verdict: the skeleton survives, "as written" does not — the Fulfiller's screen fails the frame outright, so the product deliberately carries two navigational paradigms. Kept as convention: each route declares its own layout and never reflows, deep operations push rather than cover, the URL addresses the subject.
B

The Line — the pipeline is the navigation

Refuted

"The product is a conveyor, so the UI should be the conveyor."

The nav strip deleted and replaced by a stage bar — SHOOT 85 · ANSWER 47 · PRICE 3 · SELL 597 — where the count is the navigation and the to-do list at once.

Refuted, and not on taste: Jenkins Blue Ocean was the purest stage-first UX ever shipped and is deprecated; Dagster beat Airflow by moving further toward objects; GitHub Actions ships zero counts in navigation. It also fails on its own facts — the seven stops omit the step that produces both the answer queue and the price questions, and the counts carry three different denominators.
C

The Slab — no navigation, only a subject line

Refuted

"Navigation is overhead; addressing is not."

Delete the nav, the routes and the titles. One full-bleed surface and one line that is address bar, search field and verb entry at once.

Refuted on an absence: the corpus was searched for a tool with more than one object type navigating by typed line alone and found none — Superhuman, the most keyboard-extreme product surveyed, says so in its own palette guide. And its arithmetic is fatal: deleting a 45px nav to install a 40px command line recovers 5px of 900, against 752px of measured waste.
D

The Repair — no frame, four screens fixed where they stand

Built

"Every measured defect is a decision about what sits above what, inside a column that already exists."

Two of the three zones already existed in the tree — and the 240px header and the buried photograph happened inside them. A frame cannot fix a stacking order. Four edits, no new routes.

Measured: the review card goes from 10.3% of the viewport to 32.7% — 3.17× the area — and 4,018px of scroll goes to zero. One item inside this proposal was withdrawn on the owner's correction: your #3 is wrong because 16:9 is landscape and we use portrait. The rig's camera is side-mounted, height binds, and a frame already as tall as the stage allows cannot grow when width is freed beside it.
